    Chapter 1

    What GLM-5.2 is, and the one problem it solves

    GLM-5.2 is a large language model made by Z.ai, a company in Beijing. It came out on June 13, 2026. It is open: anyone can download it and run it.

    It is built for one job above all: working on code for a very long time without losing track. Its headline feature is a context window of one million tokens. A token is a piece of a word. One million tokens is about ten long novels, or a whole software project, read in one go.

    The previous version, GLM-5.1, could hold about 200 thousand tokens. So this is five times more.

    Holding that much text is expensive. Most of the design of GLM-5.2 is about making it affordable. Keep that sentence in your head. Everything else in this film is an answer to it.

    Chapter 2

    The problem: reading is expensive

    To understand a word, a model compares it with every other word it has read. This is called attention.

    With five words, that is a small web. With twelve, the web is thick. The number of comparisons grows with the square of the number of words. Double the words, four times the work.

    At one million words, that is one trillion comparisons, on every floor of the model. Nobody can pay for that.

    So GLM-5.2 uses four shortcuts. One for the size of the brain. One for memory. One for attention. And one brand new shortcut that only GLM-5.2 has.

    Chapter 3

    The whole flow, from the outside

    Here is the whole machine at once. Text enters at the bottom. First, each token becomes a list of 6,144 numbers. Think of it as the word's identity card.

    Then the card climbs a tower of 78 floors. On every floor, the card is edited a little: it picks up context, it gets sharper.

    Floors one to three are plain floors, where everything works together. Floors four to seventy-eight are expert floors, where the card visits only a few specialists.

    At the top, the finished card is turned into a score for every word in the dictionary. The highest score is the next word. And bolted to the exit is a small helper that guesses a few words ahead.

    We will visit each of these parts.

    Chapter 4

    One floor has two rooms

    Every floor has two rooms, always in the same order.

    The first room is attention. Here the word looks around and asks: who else in this text matters to me?

    The second room is the experts. Here the word asks: now that I know my context, what do I mean?

    Gather, then think. Every floor, 78 times in a row.

    Three of the four shortcuts live in the attention room. The fourth lives in the expert room. Let's start with the expert room, because it is the easiest to picture.

    Chapter 5

    Shortcut 1 — a hospital of experts (Mixture of Experts)

    Imagine a hospital with 256 doctors. You do not see all of them. A receptionist reads your file and sends you to eight. There is also one family doctor who sees everyone.

    In GLM-5.2, the receptionist is called the router. The doctors are called experts. The family doctor is the shared expert.

    This is why the model can be huge and cheap at the same time. It has 753 billion parameters in total. That is the whole hospital. But each token only uses about 40 billion. That is the eight doctors it visits.

    The router also gives each chosen expert a weight, so their answers are blended, not just added.

    On floors one to three there is no router. Those floors use one big shared network, because early on the card is too raw to route well.

    Chapter 6

    Shortcut 2 — a smaller notebook (MLA)

    Now the attention room. When a word arrives, the model writes two things about it into a notebook: a key, which says what this word is about, and a value, which says what it carries. Later words read this notebook.

    Normally each entry is big, and at one million words the notebook is enormous. Just moving it around the chip becomes the slowest part.

    Multi-head latent attention, or MLA, fixes this. It squeezes the key and value together into one small note, called a latent. Only the small note is stored. When a later word needs it, the note is expanded back on the spot.

    Same memory of the story, much less paper. This idea comes from DeepSeek, and GLM-5.2 inherits it.

    Chapter 7

    Shortcut 3 — the highlighter (DeepSeek Sparse Attention)

    Even with small notes, reading all of them is the trillion-comparison problem from chapter two.

    DeepSeek Sparse Attention, or DSA, splits the job in two. First, a tiny cheap helper called the indexer skims every note and gives it a score. It keeps only the top 2,048. Second, the real, expensive attention reads only those 2,048 notes.

    Be precise here: the indexer still looks at every word, so it is still quadratic. But each of its looks is very cheap. The expensive part now touches 2,048 words instead of a million.

    Watch it happen: the highlighter skims first, then attention reads only the bright notes.

    Chapter 8

    Shortcut 4 — share the highlights (IndexShare, the new idea)

    Here is the catch, and here is the new idea. Every one of the 78 floors had its own indexer. Each one skims a million notes. That skimming became the biggest cost.

    Then Z.ai measured something. Neighbouring floors pick 70 to 100 percent of the same words. They keep rediscovering the same list.

    So IndexShare deletes the indexer from three out of every four floors. The pattern is: skim, copy, copy, copy, repeated up the whole tower.

    A copy floor receives the list through a wire from the floor below. Everything else it still computes itself: its own queries, its own weights, its own output. Only the list of positions is shared.

    Z.ai trained the model with this pattern built in, using sequences of 128 thousand tokens, so the surviving indexers learned to serve their neighbours.

    The result: 2.9 times less compute per token at one million context. Say it carefully in your talk: that is a compute saving, not a wall-clock speed-up. The notebook memory does not shrink by the same amount.

    Chapter 9

    The glue parts you will see on the real blueprint

    On the real blueprint you will see a few small parts we have not named yet.

    RMSNorm is a volume knob. Before each room, it rescales the card's numbers to a steady level, so nothing explodes or fades across 78 floors.

    The residual connection is a safety wire. After each room, the original card is added back to the result. So a floor can only add to what came before; it can never erase it.

    QK-Norm is a volume knob inside attention. It is applied to the queries and keys before they are compared, which keeps training stable.

    RoPE tells attention where each word sits in the text. It rotates the numbers by an amount that depends on the position.

    And SwiGLU is what each expert is made of: a small network with a gate that decides how much signal passes through. Glue, but important glue.

    Chapter 10

    Bonus shortcut — guess ahead (Multi-Token Prediction)

    One last shortcut, at the exit. Normally a model writes one word per pass through the tower. Slow.

    GLM-5.2 has a small sketcher, the multi-token prediction module, that drafts about five words ahead. The tower then checks the whole draft in one pass. Correct words are kept for free; wrong ones are thrown away. That is called speculative decoding with rejection sampling.

    GLM-5.2 adds two reuse tricks here. KVShare: the sketcher reads the tower's own notebook instead of keeping a copy. And index reuse: the sketcher's first draft step runs the highlighter once, and the later draft steps copy that list.

    Sound familiar? It is IndexShare again, at the exit door.

    In Z.ai's tests, the average accepted draft grew from 4.56 to 5.47 words. That is a 20 percent gain.

    Chapter 11

    All together: the real blueprint, part by part

    This is the same graph as hfviewer shows for amd/GLM-5.2-MXFP4 (architecture type glm_moe_dsa). Each sentence lights up the part it describes.

    Everything starts as word pieces, and the model knows exactly 154,880 of them: that is its whole dictionary. The embedding layer turns each piece into a list of 6,144 numbers, its identity card, and the card keeps this size through the entire model. RMSNorm, the volume knob, rescales the numbers to a steady level before the attention room. The kv latent projection is where MLA lives: it squeezes the word's key and value into one small note, and only the note is stored in the notebook. The indexer is the DSA highlighter: it skims every note and keeps the best 2,048, and thanks to IndexShare only one floor in four actually has this box, while the other three copy its list through a wire. Then real attention runs, reading only the 2,048 highlighted notes, with QK-Norm keeping the comparisons tame and RoPE telling it where each word sits. The plus sign is the residual connection, a safety wire that adds the original card back to whatever attention found. Volume knob number two, right before the thinking part. The router, our receptionist, reads the card, gives all 256 experts a score, and keeps the top 8. The eight chosen experts each run a small SwiGLU network while 248 stay idle, and remember that floors one to three skip the router and use one big dense network instead. The shared expert sees every word, always, so common knowledge lives there and the other experts can stay specialised. The second safety wire adds the original back again, and then the card climbs to the next floor, 78 times in total. After floor 78, one last volume knob, and then the language model head gives a score to all 154,880 dictionary words, and the highest score is the next word. The MTP module hangs off the exit: it drafts about five words ahead using the tower's own notebook, and the tower verifies the whole draft in one pass.

    Open the live version at hfviewer.com/amd/GLM-5.2-MXFP4 and you will recognise every box.
    Chapter 12

    Eight tokens without MTP: one word per pass

    Same drawing as the next chapter, but the MTP modules are switched off. Watch how many times the main model has to run.

    First, the same eight tokens the old way, with no MTP module at all. Pass 1: the prompt goes through all 78 floors and the LM head picks token 1: The. Pass 2: The goes back in at the bottom, climbs all 78 floors again, and out comes cat. Pass 3, all 78 floors again: sat. Pass 4: on. Pass 5: the. Pass 6: warm. Pass 7: mat. Pass 8: and, and we are done. Eight tokens, eight full passes through the main model, one word each time, and every pass has to wait for the one before it. Now compare with the next chapter: the same eight tokens in three main-model passes plus eight cheap MTP draft steps.

    Chapter 13

    Eight tokens, start to finish: the main model and the MTP modules together

    This is Z.ai's own architecture figure from the GLM-5.2 blog, redrawn: the main model on the left, the MTP module in the middle running its first draft step with an indexer, and the same MTP module on the right running the next draft steps without one. Each sentence lights up the part it describes, and the strip at the bottom fills up with the eight tokens.

    We want eight tokens: The cat sat on the warm mat and. Pass 1: the main model reads the whole prompt through its 78 floors, and in each group of four DSA blocks the bottom one has the indexer and computes the top-k list that the three above it reuse. The LM head turns the finished card into a score for every word, and the highest score becomes token 1: The. Now the MTP module takes over: it receives the main model's hidden state through H-Norm and the embedding of The through E-Norm, and a Linear layer joins them into one card. Its DSA block has an indexer, so it computes a fresh top-k list, and it reads the shared KV cache, the very same notebook the main model uses, so nothing is copied. The shared MTP head proposes token 2 as a draft: cat. Draft step 2 runs the MTP module again but without the indexer: it reuses the top-k list from draft step 1 and the same shared cache, and proposes sat. Draft steps 3 and 4 do the same, proposing on and a, so we have four drafts from one skim. Pass 2: the main model checks all four drafts in a single pass, comparing each draft with the word it would have chosen itself. Cat, sat and on match, so they are accepted for free, while a does not match, so it is thrown away and replaced by the main model's own choice, the. That is rejection sampling: five tokens confirmed after only two passes through the main model. Round two starts from the: the MTP module drafts warm, mat, and, purred, again one skim and three reuses. Pass 3 verifies them: warm, mat and and are accepted, and our eight tokens are done. Eight tokens for three main-model passes instead of eight, and in Z.ai's coding tests the average accepted length is 5.47 tokens per round.

    "Isn't this just DeepSeek V3.2's architecture?"

    Mostly inherited, yes: MLA, DSA and the MoE layout come from that lineage. The new contributions in 5.2 are IndexShare and the reuse tricks in the MTP module. Say it plainly; it is not a weakness.

    "Does sharing the list hurt quality?"

    Neighbouring floors already overlap 70 to 100 percent, and the pattern was trained in during mid-training at 128K tokens, so the surviving indexers adapted to their neighbours.

    "Why share across 4 floors and not all 78?"

    Overlap is high between neighbours, not between distant floors. Four is the balance Z.ai chose between savings and quality.

    "Is 2.9× the real speed-up?"

    No. It is a per-token compute estimate at 1M context. Memory for the notebook, kernels, and cache transfers still cost. The related IndexCache paper measured up to 1.82× faster prefill on a different, smaller model at 200K.

    "Where is IndexShare in the hfviewer graph?"

    Inside the sparse-attention selection step. The graph draws all 78 layers as one repeated block, so it cannot show that three of every four layers reuse a neighbour's selection; that lives in the config.

    Suggested timing for 60 minutes: intro 5, expert room 10, attention room with MLA and DSA 15, IndexShare 15, MTP 8, glue and benchmarks 7. If you must cut, cut MTP first, never IndexShare.
